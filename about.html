01 · WHERE IT STARTED

IT STARTED AS PLAY.

Before judo became my ambition, it was simply the place where I could be a kid.

I was born on 11 August 2007 in Tbilisi, Georgia—the city where I grew up and where this journey began.

I first walked into a dojo in 2015, when I was eight years old. Honestly, I had no idea what judo was or how far it could take someone. I wasn’t thinking about competitions, medals or becoming an athlete. I was an energetic kid who wanted to play, smile, burn off energy and spend time with other children my age. The dojo was the perfect place for that.

For years, that was what judo meant to me. I enjoyed training, but I wasn’t serious and didn’t yet understand the world beyond our dojo. Something changed during the COVID break in 2020. I can’t remember one exact moment when I became serious, but I remember beginning to train alone. Somewhere during that time, judo stopped being only a place to play and became something I genuinely wanted to pursue.

Since then, judo has taught me far more than how to fight. It taught me respect, discipline and patience. It gave me friendships, tested my mentality and showed me how to live with both victory and defeat. Most importantly, it taught me that meaningful progress rarely arrives quickly. If you keep showing up, eventually you look back and barely recognize the person you used to be.

I don’t know who I would have become without judo. I only know that much of the person I am today was built through thousands of hours of training, losing, learning and choosing to return.

“ You’re going to go through more than you can imagine. There will be moments when you want to stop—but keep going. In the end, it will all be worth it.

02 · WHAT IT TOOK

THE RESULT WASN’T ENOUGH.

2024 proved that I belonged. 2025 made me question whether that proof meant anything.

My first competition came near the end of spring in 2016. I was still eight years old and barely understood what competing meant. I remember repeatedly attempting Seoi-Otoshi, winning my first fight through shido and losing the second to the athlete who eventually became champion.

My first medal arrived not long afterward, in a group of four where almost everyone was going home with something. I finished second. It meant a lot to me then, but it was not the beginning of immediate success. For years, around seventy percent of my competitions ended with me losing the first fight.

That finally began to change near the end of 2023 and throughout the 2024 season. I finished fifth at the Georgian Cadet Championship in the −81 kg category. A few months later, on 28 April 2024, I entered the Telavi Cadet European Cup.

It was my first European Cup. There were eighteen athletes in my category, and I was the only one with no previous European Cup experience. I weighed around 78 kilograms, while some of my opponents were entering competition day closer to 83 or 84. Only months earlier, competing at that level had felt unimaginable.

In the final repechage round, I faced the Georgian champion—the same athlete who had defeated me in the quarterfinal of the national championship after approximately six minutes of golden score. This time, I beat him in under two minutes.

But I injured my left elbow during the first seconds of the fight. The pain became progressively worse during the break before the final block. We used ice and tape, but neither helped much. By the time I stepped onto the tatami for the bronze-medal contest, I knew my arm was badly injured.

I still fought.

My opponent had reached the semifinal and given the eventual champion a difficult fight. I defeated him in under a minute with De-Ashi-Barai—one of the cleanest attacks I have ever produced in competition. I had arrived at my first European Cup as the least experienced athlete in the category and left with a bronze medal.

After the medal ceremony, the pain became so severe that I could barely hold anything with my left arm. I could not train properly throughout May.

I believed that the result would open another door. I waited for an invitation to a national training camp or another opportunity to prove myself internationally, but it never came. I kept working throughout the summer, expecting that another chance might eventually appear.

In September, I won silver at the Black Sea Cup. In November, I travelled to the European Cup in Thessaloniki using my own money because I needed more international experience. I lost in the repechage and finished seventh. It was not the result I wanted, but after the injuries and uncertainty surrounding that period, it was still an important experience.

After Greece, my rhythm began to disappear. I struggled to return to the dojo consistently and spent much of December and the beginning of January training alone. I gained weight and moved from −81 kg to −90 kg.

My first competition in the new category was the second division of the Georgian Senior Championship in February 2025. I won my opening fight and then lost to the athlete who eventually became champion. In the repechage, I was leading by waza-ari, but my endurance in the new category failed me. I lost during the final seconds.

I built my next plan around the Georgian Junior Championship. I wanted months of structured preparation—strength, judo, cardio and speed—and I genuinely believed that I could reach the podium.

At the beginning of March, I injured my shoulder. On 4 March, a doctor told me that I should not train hard for one month and prescribed medication that caused my body to retain additional water. That evening, I went to the dojo to explain the situation to my coach. There, I learned that the championship had been moved from the beginning of June to 4 April—exactly one month away.

The month I needed to recover had become all the time I had left to prepare.

I could not train at even half of my normal intensity. I took the final dose of medication on the day of the weigh-in and had to cut approximately six kilograms—the largest weight cut I had ever attempted. I still competed. I won my first fight in around thirty seconds, but lost the second to an athlete who went on to take bronze. It was his final year as a junior. It was my first.

I later finished around seventh at the junior second division. By then, however, the problems extended far beyond competition. My weight was unstable. Injuries repeatedly interrupted important preparation periods. My personal life was falling apart, people around me were suggesting that I stop, and I was losing the spark I had already fought so many times to recover.

August was probably the lowest point of my life. September and October were not much better. I was trying to find a way forward while quietly researching how I might move to Spain, but every possible path seemed to create another problem.

Then, one Sunday morning in November, I woke up and made a decision. I would prepare for the Georgian Senior Championship harder than I had ever prepared for anything. If it worked, I would continue from there. If it did not, I would put everything I had into leaving and beginning again in Spain.

I started the preparation weighing around 95 kilograms. I did not have a perfect plan, but I committed to the process completely. I built my diet and training structure as I went, learning what my body needed through discipline, research and daily adjustment. Over the following two months, I trained harder than ever. My weight dropped, my speed returned and my cardio felt strong. By competition day, 28 December, I weighed only 86 kilograms.

Then I lost the opening fight.

During an O-Uchi-Gari attack, my head collided with my opponent and I felt as though I had been knocked down. We each scored yuko, but after approximately three minutes of golden score, he scored again. For the first time in around six years, my competition ended in the first fight.

Two months of work seemed to collapse in seconds. I returned home in complete shock. My brother had seen everything I had put into the preparation. He asked me one simple question:

“ What are you going to do now?

That was when I remembered the promise I had made to myself on that Sunday morning.

At the time, I thought 2025 was taking everything away from me. Looking back now, I see it differently. Every loss, injury, delay and unanswered question was preparing me for what came next. The year tested my belief, removed every comfortable option and forced me to decide how much this journey truly meant to me.

I am grateful for it now—not because it was easy, and not because every part of it was fair, but because I believe God used that period to prepare me. Without 2025, I might never have found the courage to take the risk that changed my life.

That loss did not end my journey. It ended my waiting.

03 · THE MOVE

I LEFT BEFORE I WAS READY.

I packed my life into thirty kilograms and left without knowing when I would come home.

On 9 February 2026, I left Georgia for Barcelona. The journey began long before the flight itself. It began with a decision to take a risk before every part of the plan was certain.

I finished packing at approximately five in the morning and slept for only one hour. My grandfather drove my brother and me to the bus station. Everything happened so quickly that my goodbye to my parents lasted only a few seconds. I left without knowing when I would see or hug my family again.

During the three-hour journey from Tbilisi to Kutaisi, my brother kept reminding me that this was the beginning of something new—perhaps something great. I was exhausted, but underneath the exhaustion were fear, excitement and the pressure of everything I was leaving behind.

At the same time, I was still thinking about the worst-case scenario: that I might have to return with my brother after two weeks. Nothing was guaranteed. I only knew that remaining where I was had stopped feeling like an option.

After waiting at the airport and spending five hours in the air, Barcelona finally appeared beneath us. When I stepped outside the plane, I began smiling without any clear reason. I had the feeling that something important was going to begin there, even though I still had no idea what it would look like.

By the time we reached where we were staying, we were too tired to understand what had just happened. The following morning, we began gathering the basic necessities required to start a new life. That evening, I walked into Judo Condal for my first training session.

The difference was immediate. In Georgia, much of my training had been built around strength, endurance and intensity. Here, the focus was technique, tactics and detail. At first, the lower intensity confused me. I wondered how I could progress like that. It took time to understand that I had arrived with physical strength, but this was the direction my judo had been missing.

I entered the dojo in some of the best physical condition of my life and attacked every randori as if I needed to prove myself immediately. My new coach repeatedly told me to slow down and remain calm. Gradually, he began giving me the technical and tactical direction I needed—new patterns, better decisions, stronger ne-waza and a clearer understanding of each fight.

His support extended beyond the tatami. He consistently made sure everything was all right and noticed when I looked tired or when something felt wrong. In a city where almost nobody knew me, that kindness made me feel that I was not completely alone.

My brother remained with me for the first two weeks. When the day came for him to leave, his final words stayed with me:

“ I will come back here, and the reason will be your success. We will all be here for you. I believe in you.

When the airport gate closed behind him, I could no longer hold back my tears. For the first time, I fully understood that I was alone in another country, far away from everyone who had always been part of my life.

That remains the hardest part of this journey. Progress does not remove the distance from my family. There are still nights when the sacrifice feels heavier than the result. But my faith has never allowed me to believe that the struggle is meaningless. I trust that God brought me through 2025 because it prepared me to survive and grow here.

Approximately two months after arriving, I competed at the Copa Catalunya Absolut and won silver. During that competition, I could feel myself progressing from one fight to the next. The technical and tactical work was already changing how I moved, thought and reacted.

After that medal, I made a promise to myself: 2025 would remain the only year I finished without a medal, and 2026 would become the year in which I won more than ever before.

My mother arrived in Barcelona on 25 April and attended my second competition on 2 May. In the semifinal, I faced an athlete from Spain’s junior national team. It was a difficult fight, but I remained patient, adapted and found a way to win. Doing it in front of my mother made it one of the most meaningful victories of my life.

That competition confirmed what the first had already begun to show me: moving was the correct decision. The person watching from the side could see the difference, but I could feel it inside every exchange. My technique, tactics, endurance, ne-waza, confidence, willingness to take risks and understanding of the fight were all changing.

Six competitions later, I had reached six podiums—three gold medals, two silver and one bronze. After the year that had tested me more than any other, I was no longer wondering what might have happened if someone had given me an opportunity. I had created one myself.

Away from the dojo, I continue learning how to build a life independently. I study web development and Spanish, manage my training and diet, and cook for myself. Sometimes I do not notice how much has changed until someone close to me reminds me.

If I could speak to my brother and myself on that first exhausted night in Barcelona, I would tell us to stop worrying and get some sleep. I would tell him that he would be proud, that the progress would arrive faster than either of us expected—and that I would miss him more than I knew.

I did not leave because I was ready. I left because waiting had already cost me enough.

04 · WHERE IT LEADS

I AM STILL BECOMING.

For the first time in years, my life is quiet—and that quiet has given me the space to build.

Most of my days now look the same. I follow the same routine, eat similar food, train, recover, study and begin again. There is not much noise around me anymore, and I have discovered that I like it that way. I finally feel at peace.

The athlete living in Barcelona is completely different from the one who left Georgia. I am more technical, more patient and more capable of understanding what is happening inside a fight. But I am still far from complete.

My dominant left grip is one of my greatest strengths. When I establish it properly, I want my opponent to feel that the fight has already changed—that a score is coming and escaping the pressure will be almost impossible. But when someone successfully prevents or breaks that grip, I can sometimes become disorganized and fail to express the rest of my judo. That is one of the weaknesses I am determined to remove.

I do not want to depend on one position, one attack or one plan. I want to become the athlete who always has another answer.

I want my judo to be remembered for its mental strength, footwork, technique, pressure and fight intelligence. I want opponents to fear my left grip, but I also want them to know that stopping it will not be enough. My ambition is to build a complete style—one that can adapt, think and continue applying pressure regardless of what happens during the match.

My immediate goal is to earn the opportunity to compete at the Spanish Junior Championship and win it. The regulations currently make that opportunity uncertain, but I hope I will be given the chance to prove myself on the tatami.

I believe I belong among the highest level of junior judokas in the world. Within Spain, I believe I am already capable of controlling these contests decisively. I do not want that confidence to remain something I only say to myself. I want my performances to prove it. Every remaining junior competition is another opportunity to demonstrate my level, sharpen my judo and continue the podium streak I began after arriving here.

The next step is not the final destination. First come the national competitions and European Cups. After that come the Grand Prix, Grand Slams, European Championships and World Championships. At the end of that road stands the largest dream of all: an Olympic gold medal.

I do not expect to arrive there immediately. I want to earn every stage of the journey before moving to the next one.

My hope is to one day earn the right to represent Spain internationally. Spain is where I found the environment, direction and opportunity that allowed my judo to grow again. Representing this country would mean far more than wearing its name on my back. It would prove that the risk of coming here became something real.

It would also prove that the people who believed in me were not wrong.

My goal is to build a life that allows me to devote myself completely to judo. My ideal life is not complicated: wake up, train, eat healthy food, recover properly, sleep well and repeat the process without unnecessary distractions. To some people, that life might appear repetitive. To me, it would be freedom—the opportunity to give everything I have to becoming the athlete I know I can be.

I already train twice a day when circumstances allow it, but I want that discipline to become completely consistent. I want this journey to strengthen every part of me: my mentality, physical ability, independence, character and the way I handle responsibility.

I am prepared to sacrifice comfort, convenience and certainty. But I do not want success to take away the person I am trying to become. I want to remain a good person, a source of support and someone whose value exists beyond the results written beside his name.

Faith is what allows me to think beyond what currently appears possible. It turns dreams into goals and goals into destinations. I believe that honest work is never wasted. It may not always return in the form you expected, but the discipline, sacrifice and hours eventually become part of you. The more you genuinely put into something, the more it gives back—even if the reward arrives somewhere you did not predict.

Outside judo, web development remains one of my strongest interests. I have enjoyed computers since childhood, and when I discovered coding and design, they felt natural to me. Learning Spanish, building this website and managing an independent life are not distractions from my athletic journey. They are different parts of the same person I am becoming.

One day, when my competitive career is over, I would like to share what I learned with younger athletes. Perhaps I will coach, work with a national team or create the kind of dojo in which athletes have everything they need to develop properly. Time will decide the exact form, but I know I want my experience to become useful to someone after me.

I want my name to be remembered among the greatest athletes to practise this sport. But I do not want medals to be the only reason people remember me. I want those who knew me outside the tatami to remember a good person and someone who supported the people around him.

Success would make the injuries, lonely nights and sacrifices worth enduring. But its deepest meaning would not come from keeping every achievement for myself. It would come from being able to return something to the people whose belief carried me when my own strength was not enough.

Ten years from now, I hope I can look back at the person I am today and thank him for refusing to abandon himself. I know the road can become harder than anything I have already experienced. My promise is not that I will always win. My promise is that I will always try—that difficulty will never be enough to make me lose faith or stop giving my best.

I will know that I made it when the people who carried me can look at the life I built and know that none of their belief was wasted.
